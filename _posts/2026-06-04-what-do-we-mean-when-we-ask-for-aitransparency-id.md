<p>What do we mean when we ask for #AItransparency?</p>
<p>I’d seen some examples and decided to collect them, inspired by the effort by Scott McCarthy [1] and James Barnard on AI disclosure icons for #medialocalization. There's been an interesting discussion around the idea, showing how many different things we may be trying to disclose.</p>
<p>One thing is “AI was used”, but some argue that the where and how are more important: in the script, translation, subtitles, voice, music, image, performance, lip movements? As a first draft, a tool, a replacement? With consent, credit, and fair pay?</p>
<p>A few initiatives I’ve come across:</p>
<p>[2] #VerifiedHuman proposes a five-level Human-AI Spectrum: entirely human, human-created AI-enhanced, human-led AI-assisted, AI-led human-directed, and entirely AI-generated. It's all about “who led the creative process”.</p>
<p>[3] #HumanAuthored, from the Society of Authors and the Authors Guild, focuses on books and allows writers to identify works created by humans rather than generated by AI. Books are a more contained case than audiovisual media, but the principle is the same: human authorship may increasingly need to be made visible, because it used to be assumed.</p>
<p>[4] #HUMACertificate uses categories such as Human Origin, AI-Assisted Human Work, Human-AI Collaboration, and AI Origin, Human Curated. Again, it avoids a simple yes/no answer.</p>
<p>There is also the broader discussion around “human-made” or “AI-free” logos (see BBC article [5]). And I have written before [6] that translator credits already function, indirectly, as a sign of human-made translation, because they leave a human trace of accountability.</p>
<p>But a logo only helps if people know what it means. “Human-made” sounds simple, but when humans use tools, where do we draw the line? Does spellcheck count? A terminology database? Machine translation? What if the translation is human-made but the voice is synthetic?</p>
<p>Audiovisual work makes this especially visible because the final product is made of many layers. Text, image, voice, performance, timing, music, accessibility, adaptation, and localization can each have different workflows and different degrees of automation.</p>
<p>For translators, subtitlers, dubbing writers, voice actors, and other localization professionals, this matters because the work was already invisible before AI. We always strived for invisible translations, and in the process we forgot that the people should not be invisible.</p>
