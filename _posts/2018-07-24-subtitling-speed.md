<p>#Subtitling speed<br>
When watching films in English, young, well-educated viewers proficient<br>
in English are able to follow #subtitles displayed at a speed of up to 20<br>
characters per second (cps). They prefer text to be less condensed in English-language films <a href="https://avt.ils.uw.edu.pl/files/2018/07/SURE_Project_Report.pdf">https://avt.ils.uw.edu.pl/files/2018/07/SURE_Project_Report.pdf</a></p>
{% endraw %}

{% raw %}
<p>When watching foreign films in an unknown language, most people prefer<br>
dialogue to be condensed and subtitles displayed at a slower speed.<br>
Subtitles displayed at slow speed (12 cps) result in more re-reading.</p>
{% endraw %}

{% raw %}
<p>When watching English-language films with slow subtitles (12 cps), viewers<br>
tend to notice discrepancies between the dialogue and the subtitles,<br>
resulting from text reduction.</p>
{% endraw %}

{% raw %}
<p>Subtitle segmentation (line breaks)<br>
Linguistic units should be kept together and should not be split across<br>
the lines.<br>
Non-syntactically segmented text in the subtitles may increase cognitive<br>
load and induce more re-reading.</p>
{% endraw %}

{% raw %}
<p>Subtitles should not have more than two lines.<br>
(And my favorite:) It is more important to keep closely related linguistic units together than to create a certain subtitle shape, like a pyramid or rectangle.</p>
{% endraw %}
